\begin{abstract}What do averages and snapshots hide about inequality, relationships and change over time? We study country internet indicators and daily US weather separately. Network, multivariable, temporal and spatial views connect each question to measured evidence. Broadband and internet use correlate strongly across country levels (0.831), but weakly across their changes (0.054). About 47\% of measured adoption variation lies within regions. Hot-dry conditions occupy 16.27\% of weighted grid-cell days, yet local runs reach 67 days. These contrasts show why group summaries, endpoints and separate frequencies need individual values, histories and shared timing for interpretation.\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}country internet use, visual comparison, temporal change, daily weather, multivariable visualization\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

A national adoption rate hides infrastructure differences; a regional mean hides its member countries; a monthly weather summary hides daily events. We use linked comparisons to recover that missing detail.

\textbf{Central question: What do averages and snapshots hide about inequality, relationships and change over time?}

We ask: (1) Do similar internet-use levels conceal different telecom profiles? (2) Does overall progress imply catch-up among the same countries? (3) What do weather averages and selected dates omit about location, event timing and duration? Country data answer the first two questions; the separate weather investigation answers the third. Each result is interpreted as a descriptive pattern in the included data, with its scope stated.



\section{Data and preparation}

\textbf{Country inputs and cleaning.} Three A1 OWID/ITU CSV extracts \cite{ref2} provide internet-user counts, internet-use percentages and telecom subscriptions. \path{scripts/preprocess_data.py} renames columns, checks source-key uniqueness and numeric types, then outer-joins the files on entity, country code and year with one-to-one validation. The join has 13,387 rows; removing 823 explicitly listed aggregate rows leaves 12,564 country-year records. Missing values remain missing: they are neither replaced with zero nor imputed. A separate code-based lookup supplies regions; unmapped entries remain Unclassified.

\textbf{Comparable cohorts and transformations.} Each task declares its complete cases: 179 countries with all four 2020 profile indicators for the network; the same 175 countries in both 2015 and 2020 for paired changes; 159 countries with all 21 annual adoption values for 2000-2020; and 184 countries with user counts and adoption for treemaps. A change is computed within country before summarizing. Internet use is a percentage of people; landline, broadband and mobile values are subscriptions per 100 people and may exceed 100. These units are retained in profiles and changes.

\textbf{Professor's guidance and network construction.} Following the professor's advice, we score every country pair, inspect the similarity distribution and filter highly similar pairs. Four indicators are z-scored separately across the 179 countries: subtract the indicator's country mean and divide by its standard deviation. Euclidean distance d gives similarity s=1/(1+d). Our team chooses the 95th percentile, P95=0.569763, as the sparse default: 797 of 15,931 pairs remain, with all 179 nodes retained. P95 is our team's choice. Fig.~\ref{fig:1} explains that choice and its sensitivity.

\textbf{Weather period and sampling rationale.} gridMET \cite{ref1} supplies \path{tmmx} (daily maximum temperature), \path{pr} (rain), \path{vs} (wind speed at 10 m) and \path{th} (wind FROM-direction at 10 m). July-September spans peak summer into early autumn, allowing heat persistence, cooling and rain timing to be compared in one contiguous interval. The selected 2023 year includes independently documented Southwest July heat \cite{ref9}, making persistence substantively relevant; it is a case study, not a representative climate year. Uniform eight-date sampling, 13 days apart with both endpoints, gives reproducible seasonal coverage without the uneven gaps possible in a small random sample. It cannot ensure capture of short extremes; all 92 days support monthly/event inferences.

\textbf{Weather preparation.} Decode NetCDF packing/masks before converting source Kelvin to Celsius; supplied caches are already Celsius. Read every sixth grid cell (subsampling, not averaging), align coordinates/dates and retain the same 13,416 cells valid for all variables on all days. Cosine-latitude weights approximate area. Rain stays mm/day, speed m/s and direction degrees FROM north; quiver components point toward flow. The audit records hashes, missing counts, cohorts and sampled-to-daily checks.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The separate bars give about 18.06\% hot, 76.65\% dry and 11.65\% windy weighted cell-days. \section{Discussion}

Country averages conceal profile differences (71 of 454 nearly equal-adoption pairs meet P95), different paired paths, and the 47\% within-region variation. Broadband association weakens from 0.83 for levels to 0.05 for gains, with ceiling/diffusion effects limiting interpretation. Layouts preserve edges while changing crossing costs; omitting one indicator gives Jaccard 0.338-0.512, exposing feature sensitivity despite small-noise stability.

Weather comparisons recover different omitted details: endpoint signs disagree with monthly means over 18.26\% of area; totals conceal concentrated rainfall; equal 75-day event counts produce 17- versus 67-day runs. Separate field frequencies also fail to recover the 2.57\% joint occurrence. These findings answer the main question through profiles, matched change and daily order.



\textbf{Scope and limits.} Country aggregates support descriptive comparisons, not causal attribution. Complete-case cohorts exclude missing observations; counts, shares and subscription rates use different denominators. Weather results concern one three-month interval and a fixed subsampled footprint, with approximate area weights and dependent cells/days. Daily fields need not coincide hourly; runs may extend beyond the window. Event cutoffs are descriptive, and period departures are not climate normals. Stride six reduces processing volume and display overlap; no native-resolution equivalence is claimed.
